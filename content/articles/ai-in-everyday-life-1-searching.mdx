*This is the first chapter of a ten-part series on how I use AI in everyday life. One chapter a week, one real use case each time, with what worked, what failed, and the rule I kept.*

## The first answer is a draft

Most people use an AI assistant the way they used a search engine: type a question, read the answer, close the tab.

That is the least interesting way to use it.

The first answer is a draft. It is a reasonable starting point built from your wording, your assumptions and whatever the system retrieved on its first pass. It tells you where the territory is. It does not tell you what the territory looks like.

The value appears at the second question, the third, the seventh. That is where a conversation stops being a lookup and becomes an investigation.

In [the previous article](/articles/ai-search-everywhere-optimization-seo-geo-aeo-2026) I looked at this shift from the side of the publisher: how brands become sources that AI systems can find and trust. This chapter looks at the other side of the screen. What changes when you are the one asking?

## What searching used to cost

Think about what a serious search session looked like not long ago.

You typed a keyword query. You opened five or six tabs. You skimmed each one for the part that mattered. You noticed that two sources contradicted each other, so you opened two more. You rewrote the query because the vocabulary was wrong. You discovered a term you did not know, searched for that, and started again.

The cost was never the searching itself. It was everything around it:

- Finding the right words for a question you did not fully understand yet.
- Reading pages to extract one useful paragraph.
- Reconciling sources that disagree.
- Keeping the thread of what you had already learned.
- Looking at the problem from more than one angle, which almost nobody did, because each new angle meant starting a new search.

That last point is the important one. With a search engine, a different perspective was expensive, so we usually settled for one.

## The loop I actually use

When the question matters, I do not accept the first answer. I iterate. The loop is simple, and I run it almost by reflex now.

**1. Ask the question in plain language.**
No keywords. The full question, with the context that matters: who I am, what I am trying to decide, what constraints I have.

**2. Challenge the answer.**
"What is the strongest argument against this?" "Who would disagree, and why?" A first answer usually sounds more settled than the topic really is.

**3. Change the angle.**
Same subject, different lens. Cost, risk, time horizon, a beginner's view, an expert's view, the view of someone who has already made the mistake.

**4. Sharpen the question.**
By now I know the vocabulary and the real shape of the problem. The question I ask at step four is much better than the one I asked at step one.

**5. Verify what matters.**
Ask for sources. Open them. Check the numbers, the dates, the claims that would change my decision.

None of these steps is clever. The leverage comes from how cheap each one is. A new angle used to cost a new search session. Now it costs one sentence.

## A worked example

Here is the kind of sequence I run. The subject below is deliberately ordinary: choosing a laptop for work. The pattern is the same whatever you are researching.

**Round 1: the naive question.**

```text
I write code and do light video editing. Which laptop should I buy?
```

The answer is generic and predictable: a short list of popular models with the usual specifications. It is useful as a map, and nothing more. Notice that it answers the question I asked, not the one I should have asked.

**Round 2: challenge the framing.**

```text
What are you assuming about my needs that I haven't told you?
What would change your recommendation?
```

This is one of my favorite questions. It exposes the hidden variables: budget, operating system, how often I travel, whether I plug in at a desk or work on the move, how long I keep a machine. Half of the useful insight is in discovering what I had not decided yet.

**Round 3: change the time horizon.**

```text
Assume I keep this laptop for six years. How does the ranking change?
Focus on repairability, battery replacement and software support.
```

The ranking moves. Cheap and thin stops looking attractive once repair costs and support windows enter the picture. A search engine could tell me all of this, but only if I had known which six queries to write.

**Round 4: ask for the opposition.**

```text
Argue against the option you ranked first. Be specific.
```

A good answer here is worth more than the original recommendation, because it tells me which risks I am accepting.

**Round 5: find out where the experts disagree.**

```text
Which points in this decision are genuinely contested, and which are settled?
```

This separates facts from taste. It saves me from spending an evening on a debate that has no right answer.

**Round 6: verify and close.**

```text
Give me the sources for the claims about battery life and support
duration, with dates. Flag anything that comes from a manufacturer.
```

Then I open the sources. This is the step that keeps the whole process honest.

Six questions. Six different axes: assumptions, time, risk, opposition, consensus, evidence. With a classic search engine, I would have needed a new query for each one, plus the reading, plus the effort of remembering what the previous tabs said.

## The axes worth switching between

Over time I have built a small repertoire of angles. When an answer feels too smooth, I pick one:

- **The skeptic.** What is the strongest argument against this?
- **The beginner.** Explain this as if I had never met the topic. Where do newcomers go wrong?
- **The expert.** What would a specialist add that a generalist would miss?
- **The time horizon.** What changes in one month, one year, five years?
- **The money.** What is the total cost, not the price tag?
- **The failure case.** How does this go wrong, and how often?
- **The alternative.** What am I not considering?
- **The disagreement.** Where do serious people disagree, and why?
- **The meta question.** What should I have asked that I did not?

The last one is, in my experience, the most productive. "What should I be asking that I am not?" turns the assistant from an answering machine into a research partner.

## Where the time is actually gained

I want to be careful here, because "AI saves time" is the kind of claim that deserves suspicion. I am not going to give you a percentage. I did not measure it, and anyone who gives you a precise figure without a method is selling something.

What I can describe is where the time goes.

It is saved on **exploration**: finding the vocabulary, understanding the shape of a topic, seeing several perspectives, and keeping the thread across a long session. This is the part of research that used to be slow and tedious, and it is the part that has improved the most.

It is **not** saved on **verification**. Checking that a claim is true, that a source says what it is supposed to say, that a price is current, still takes the time it takes. If you skip it, you have not saved time. You have borrowed it, with interest.

So the honest summary is this: AI makes it much cheaper to understand a question deeply, and about as expensive as before to prove an answer is right. The skill is knowing which of the two you are doing at any moment.

## What goes wrong

Iterating helps, but it does not remove the failure modes. These are the ones I watch for.

**Confident mistakes.** A wrong answer reads exactly like a right one. Fluency is not evidence. Anything that affects a real decision gets checked against a source I can open.

**Agreement with my own framing.** These systems tend to work with the premise you hand them. If I ask "why is option A better?", I will get reasons why A is better. So I ask neutral questions, and I ask for the opposite case explicitly.

**Stale or missing information.** Prices, availability, regulations, opening hours and software versions change. When freshness matters, I make sure the answer comes with dated sources, or I go to the primary one directly.

**Invented references.** A citation that looks plausible is not a citation that exists. I open the links. If a source cannot be found, the claim it supported is dropped.

**Losing the thread.** In a long session it is easy to drift away from the original question. I periodically ask for a short summary of where we are and what is still unresolved.

## Search engines are not obsolete

I still use a classic search engine, often.

When I need a specific page, an official document, a price, a map, a timetable or a primary source, it is faster and more reliable to go straight there. A search engine is excellent at finding things. An AI assistant is excellent at helping me think about them.

Increasingly the two blur together, since many AI systems now search the web as part of an answer. Google describes this behavior as [query fan-out](https://developers.google.com/search/docs/appearance/ai-features): the system runs several related searches across subtopics and combines the results. In a sense, the assistant is doing the multi-tab work I used to do by hand, and doing it in parallel.

That is exactly why the human part matters more, not less. The system can run many queries. It cannot know which of the questions are the important ones. That remains my job.

## The rule I kept

If I had to compress this chapter into one rule, it would be this:

**Treat the first answer as the beginning of the research, not the end of it.**

And if I had to keep a checklist, it would be short:

1. Give context, not keywords.
2. Ask what is being assumed.
3. Ask for the case against.
4. Change the angle at least three times.
5. Ask what I should have asked.
6. Verify the two or three claims that carry the decision.

That takes a few minutes, and it gets me to a better-informed decision than an hour of tabs used to.
