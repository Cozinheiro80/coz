## The email that sounds like nobody

You have seen it. A message arrives, polite and well structured, with a warm opening line, three tidy paragraphs and a closing sentence offering to "connect further". Nothing in it is wrong. Nothing in it sounds like the person who sent it.

You can usually tell within two lines. Not because the text is bad, but because it is smooth in a way people are not. No hesitation, no shortcut, no private joke, no slightly odd word choice that only that colleague would use.

Last week was about search, where the AI helps you think. Writing is harder, because here the AI does not only help you think. It can produce the final artifact. The text exists, it is fluent, and sending it takes one click.

That is the risk. Writing is where you are most tempted to outsource the part that was never meant to be outsourced.

## What writing is actually for

An email, a message, a proposal: each one is a small piece of work with two jobs.

The first job is **to transmit information**. A date, a decision, a request, a number. A machine can do this very well.

The second job is **to carry a relationship**. Tone, care, directness, the way you handle bad news, the amount of warmth you show a person you have worked with for five years. This is the part that makes a message yours.

When I ask whether AI should write something, I ask which job dominates.

- A meeting reschedule, a confirmation, a reminder: mostly job one. I am comfortable letting AI take most of the work.
- A reply to a colleague who is stressed, a message that declines something, feedback, an apology: mostly job two. The words are the relationship. I write these myself, and I use AI around the edges, if at all.

Most real messages sit in between. That is where having a method matters.

## Where AI helps me, and where it does not

After a few months of paying attention, I can name the moments where it earns its place.

**Getting unstuck.** The blank page is the most expensive part of writing. I paste in a few messy bullets and ask for a rough first structure. I rarely keep the result. What I keep is the momentum: now there is something on the page I can disagree with.

**Tightening.** I write the draft, then ask for the same text a third shorter, or ask it to flag every sentence that carries no information. I compare the two versions and take back what I miss.

**Checking the tone.** "Read this as the person receiving it. How does it land? Is anything ambiguous or sharper than I intended?" This is one of the most useful things I do. It gives me a reader's reaction before the real reader has one.

**Translating and adjusting register.** Writing in a second language, or moving from a casual team message to something a client will read. AI is good at this, as long as I read the result carefully enough to notice when it has changed what I meant.

**Catching mistakes.** Typos, missing attachments mentioned but not attached, a date that does not match the day of the week. It is a patient proofreader.

And the moments where it does not:

**Anything where I have not decided what I think yet.** If my own position is vague, the AI will produce something confident and plausible, and I will be tempted to adopt it. The text then states an opinion I never formed. I now settle the position first, in a sentence or two of my own, and only then ask for help with the wording.

**Sensitive and personal messages.** A condolence, a difficult conversation, a negotiation with someone I know well. A fluent paragraph here is worse than a clumsy sentence that is clearly mine.

**Anything with facts I cannot check.** The model will happily write "as we discussed last Tuesday" about a meeting that never happened. It does not know. I do.

## The method: draft first, assistant second

The single most useful habit I have adopted is a small change of order.

**I do not start from the AI's draft. I start from mine.**

Even if mine is three lines of bad prose, it contains the decisions: what I want to say, what I want from the reader, what I refuse to say. The AI can improve the wording. It cannot supply the intent.

Here is the sequence I follow for anything that matters.

**1. Write the intent in plain words.**
Not the email. The point. "I want to move the deadline by a week, I want them to accept without feeling blamed, and I don't want to explain the internal reason."

**2. Write a rough draft myself.**
Ugly is fine. It will be reshaped, but the decisions in it stay.

**3. Ask for help on one thing at a time.**
Shorter. Clearer. More direct. Less defensive. One instruction per pass. If I ask for "better", I get an average of every email the model has ever seen.

**4. Compare, then choose.**
I read the new version next to mine and take what is genuinely an improvement. Often it is two or three phrases, not the whole text.

**5. Read it aloud before sending.**
If a sentence sounds like something I would never say, it goes. The ear is a very good detector of borrowed voice.

## A worked example

The situation is ordinary: I need to tell a client that a deliverable will arrive a week late.

**My rough draft.**

```text
Hi Claire, quick heads up: the report won't be ready Friday, more
like next Friday. The data cleaning took longer than planned.
I'd rather give you a good report a week late than a shaky one
on time. Can we keep our call on Thursday to go through what we
already have?
```

It is direct and a bit blunt, but it contains the decisions: honest about the delay, a reason, a clear preference for quality, a concrete next step.

**What happens if I just ask "write an email telling a client the report is late".**

```text
Dear Claire,

I hope this message finds you well. I am writing to inform you
that, due to unforeseen challenges encountered during the data
preparation phase, the delivery of the report will unfortunately
be delayed. We sincerely apologize for any inconvenience this
may cause and appreciate your understanding. Please do not
hesitate to reach out should you have any questions.

Kind regards
```

Every sentence is acceptable. Together they say less than my draft. The reason is gone, the plan is gone, the call is gone, and "unforeseen challenges" tells Claire nothing. It also sounds like a stranger wrote it.

**What I do instead.**

```text
Here is my draft. Don't rewrite it. Point out anything that could
sound defensive or unclear to the client, and suggest one small
change for each.
```

The useful result is two or three comments: that "quick heads up" might make a week sound smaller than it is, and that I should say what she will have on Thursday. I apply those changes in my own words. The message stays mine and becomes better.

The difference is not the tool. It is the order of operations.

## Keeping your voice

A voice is made of habits. How long your sentences run. Whether you open with a greeting or go straight in. The words you reach for and the ones you would never use. Your tolerance for adverbs.

AI output drifts toward an average of all of them, and the average is nobody's voice. A few practices help me pull it back.

- **Give it samples.** I paste in two or three real messages I wrote and ask it to match that style. This works better than adjectives like "friendly but professional", which produce exactly the average I am trying to avoid.
- **Name what to avoid.** My own list: no "I hope this message finds you well", no "please do not hesitate", no "delve", no exclamation marks in client mail. Keep a list of yours.
- **Edit last, always.** The final pass is by me, by hand, even if it is only a minute. This is where small personal marks go back in.
- **Notice the tells.** Triplets of adjectives, balanced "not only... but also" sentences, every paragraph closing on a neat little moral. When I see these in my own text, I know a pass went too far.

## What goes wrong

**Fluent emptiness.** The text reads well and says nothing specific. A good test: could this message be sent, unchanged, to any other person in the same situation? If so, it is not finished.

**Invented details.** Dates, names, past conversations, figures. The model fills gaps with plausible content. I check every concrete detail against what I actually know.

**Tone mismatch.** Too formal for a colleague, too casual for a first contact, too enthusiastic for bad news. I ask for a reader's reaction instead of trusting my own judgment of tone, which is often wrong when I am close to the subject.

**The recipient notices.** People increasingly recognize machine-sounding text, and many discount it. A message that looks automated says something about how much attention the sender gave. That cost is real, even if it is invisible to you.

**Confidential material.** Pasting a client's email, a contract clause or personal data into a tool you are not cleared to use is a different problem from style. Before I paste anything that is not mine, I check whether the tool and the content are allowed to meet. If not, I describe the situation in generic terms or anonymize it first.

## A note on honesty

There is a question underneath all this that I do not think has a single answer: do you owe people a disclosure when AI helped with a message?

My own working position is modest. For everyday correspondence, I treat AI like a spell checker or a colleague who read over my shoulder: the message is mine, because I decided what it says and I stand behind every line. For anything where the reader would reasonably expect that I personally wrote it, or where rules require disclosure, I say so or I write it myself.

The test I use is simple. If the person found out exactly how the message was produced, would they feel misled? If yes, I change how I do it.

## The rule I kept

If I had to compress this chapter into one sentence:

**Decide what you mean first. Let AI help you say it. Never let it decide.**

And the checklist stays short:

1. Write the intent in plain words before anything else.
2. Start from my own draft, even a bad one.
3. Ask for one improvement at a time.
4. Check every date, name and figure the text contains.
5. Read it aloud. Remove whatever does not sound like me.
6. Keep the hardest messages for my own hands.
